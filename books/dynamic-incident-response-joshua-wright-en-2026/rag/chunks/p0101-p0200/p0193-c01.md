DOCUMENTATION
Documenting an incident as it unfolds produces a more accurate record than writing the documentation
after the fact. Analysts naturally defer documentation under the pressure of active response. However,
memories of specific commands, timestamps, and decisions fade once the response effort concludes, and
reconstructing them later often produces an incomplete or misleading record. The incident response team
should  treat documentation as a continuous  practice  that begins  at  verification and accumulates
throughout the response actions loop, rather than a task deferred to the end of the incident.

          Documenting as the incident progresses produces a better record and breaks the tedious
           documentation task into smaller, more manageable pieces across the incident response
            process.

Using the documentation platform established during the incident preparation activity for incident tracking
(see Identify a Platform for Incident Tracking), the incident response analyst should capture information
about the incident. This will begin an information-collection process that will continue throughout the
incident’s lifecycle. Information to collect during the verification activity may include:

  • Incident identifier: A unique identifier for the incident, often generated by the incident tracking
    platform.
  • Incident title: A descriptive title for the incident, often based on the source of the report or the incident
    classification. The title serves as a quick, intuitive reference for the team when referring to the incident.
  • Handler ID: The identifier for the incident response analyst responsible for assessing the incident.
  • Incident summary: A brief description of the incident, including the source of the report, the date and
   time of the report, and any other relevant information.
  • Incident classification: An  initial incident classification, based on the information available. Some
    incident  classifications might be unauthorized  access,  phishing,  malicious  code,  sensitive  data
    disclosure, account compromise, public-facing exploit, and more.
  • Evidence references: References to evidence collected during the detect activity.

The documentation started during the verification activity is usually used throughout the incident response
process to track the incident, document response actions, and provide a record for future reference.
Further, it can be used by the incident response team to provide updates to decision makers and other
stakeholders as the incident response process progresses. Later, this documentation will be used to create a
final incident report or as part of an organization’s cyber insurance claim.

At this point in the process, however, there is often a feeling of urgency to move quickly. While this is
important, it is also important to document the incident accurately, with sufficient detail that it is useful for
subsequent activities in the incident response process. A modern adage is that if you are typing too fast to
take notes, you are just going too fast. Taking the time to document the incident also helps moderate the
investigation’s pace, ensuring that the team does not move so quickly that it misses important details.

   Analysts should maintain the integrity of incident documentation throughout the incident response
   process. Contamination can compromise the  validity  of documentation as evidence  in  legal
